\begin{abstract}
EEG foundation models seek transferable representations of heterogeneous
multivariate time series, but downstream accuracy alone does not establish
that their latent features capture task-relevant physiology. Random masking,
reconstruction targets, and the coupling of spatial and temporal learning
may preserve neural information alongside subject and acquisition effects.
This study examines eight pretrained models and a compact supervised EEGNet
baseline. Data preparation covers 14 EEG corpora spanning clinical,
motor-imagery, affective, sleep, and speech-related tasks; the reported
comparisons concern four-class BCI Competition IV-2a, with additional REVE
similarity diagnostics on FACED and PhysioNet motor imagery.
Average-pooled frozen features yield 26.80--33.31\% balanced accuracy with
a linear SVM, against a 25\% chance reference. Under a separate
subject-disjoint protocol, a common token-preserving readout reaches
65.21\% with REVE, while EEGNet, with approximately 3,000 parameters,
reaches 76.77\%. Native channels favor CBraMod and EEGMamba, whereas mapping
favors EEGNet. REVE visualizations exhibit subject-associated organization,
weak class contrast, and lower held-out energy capture by leading components;
these observations do not identify artifacts or prove that task information
is absent. Different subject splits, readout capacities, and input adaptation
prevent attributing score differences solely to pooling. The findings
motivate stronger physiological validation, subject-level replication, and
controls for high-dimensional representation metrics. Waveform reconstruction
is an imperfect surrogate for physiological relevance, but latent alignment
alone is not sufficient either. Non-contrastive joint-embedding methods such
as LeJEPA, potentially combined with reconstruction, merit controlled
comparison using augmentations that vary nuisance components while preserving
neural phenomena. Progress should be judged by interpretable and robust
cross-subject transfer, rather than model scale or a single decoding score.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Electroencephalography (EEG) is a multivariate time series in which temporal
dynamics and relationships between electrodes jointly carry information.
A useful general-purpose representation should retain task-relevant activity
across participants, sessions, electrode layouts, and recording contexts.
Large-scale self-supervised pretraining aims to reduce dependence on labels,
but transfer requires more than reconstructing observations or obtaining a
high score on one dataset. The central question is which aspects of the
signal are preserved and remain useful under changes in the data distribution.

\subsection{Foundation Models and Spatiotemporal Learning}

The comparison includes eight models: CBraMod, EEGMamba, CSBrain,
LuMamba, LUNA, LaBraM, EEGPT, and REVE. These models represent different approaches to combining spatial and
temporal information, rather than a single homogeneous class of encoders.
EEGNet provides a compact supervised comparator~\cite{lawhern2018eegnet}.
Its task-specific training also distinguishes the value of pretrained
features from the performance achievable by learning directly from labels.

LaBraM models channel--time patch sequences with a Transformer and predicts
discrete codes from a tokenizer trained through neural spectrum prediction.
Its targets therefore differ from direct waveform samples~\cite{jiang2024labram}.
CBraMod instead separates spatial and temporal interactions into parallel
criss-cross attention mechanisms, with positional encoding adapted to the
two axes, and reconstructs masked signal patches~\cite{wang2025cbramod}.
CSBrain extends multiscale modeling through temporal windows, anatomical
regions, and structured sparse attention linking these scales~\cite{zhou2025csbrain}.

EEGPT combines local spatiotemporal encoding with representation alignment
to a momentum encoder and a masked reconstruction objective. It consequently
already couples signal-level supervision with latent targets~\cite{wang2024eegpt}.
REVE uses Transformer processing with positional information that combines
three-dimensional electrode coordinates and temporal position. Its masked
autoencoding uses contiguous spatiotemporal blocks, rather than only
independently missing patches~\cite{elouahidi2025reve}.

EEGMamba uses a bidirectional state-space backbone to model dependencies
among EEG patches. Its pretraining objective is masked
reconstruction~\cite{wang2025eegmamba}.
LUNA first unifies variable electrode sets through learned-query
cross-attention, then applies temporal Transformer processing in a fixed
latent space~\cite{doner2025luna}. LuMamba uses this channel-unification
principle with bidirectional Mamba processing and studies reconstruction,
LeJEPA, and their combination~\cite{broustail2026lumamba}. Thus, joint patch
modeling, separate axial attention, multiscale regional processing, and
spatial compression before temporal modeling impose different assumptions
about how EEG dependencies should be learned.

\subsection{What Should Self-Supervision Preserve?}

Random masking is a common pretraining mechanism: selected patches are
hidden and inferred from the observed context. The prediction target can
be a waveform, a discrete code, or a learned representation. Mask location,
extent, and spatial organization determine which dependencies are useful
for this task. Independent masks can leave nearby temporal samples or
correlated channels visible; structured masks can demand broader context.
Neither choice guarantees that successful prediction requires the
physiological phenomenon relevant to a downstream task.

Waveform reconstruction is particularly difficult to interpret as a
physiological learning criterion. EEG measurements combine multiple sources,
and task-relevant rhythms or transients need not dominate sample-wise error.
For a squared reconstruction loss, larger residual amplitudes receive more
weight, regardless of their neural or artifactual origin. Predictable
background activity or contamination can therefore be useful for minimizing
the objective. Conversely, reproducing the exact phase of a waveform may
not be necessary for a task defined by a spectral or spatial pattern.
These are reasons to question reconstruction loss as a surrogate for
physiological relevance, not to dismiss temporal signals as uninformative.

Latent-space objectives change the target but do not automatically solve
this problem. LeJEPA aligns related views and uses distributional
regularization to oppose collapse without explicit negative
pairs~\cite{balestriero2025lejepa}. Contrastive methods such as TS2Vec
separately demonstrate the potential of view-based learning for multivariate
time series~\cite{yue2022ts2vec}. In EEG, LuMamba reports benefits from
combining LeJEPA with reconstruction~\cite{broustail2026lumamba}; this argues
for comparing complementary objectives rather than presuming that latent
alignment universally supersedes signal reconstruction.

Across these approaches, agreement can preserve subject identity or
acquisition effects if both views share them. Spatial encoding likewise
provides a useful inductive bias without guaranteeing invariance to montage
or reference changes. Recent audits document subject-related structure and
limitations of robustness and interpretability in EEG foundation
models~\cite{lin2026identity,sirca2026beyond}. Physiological analyses also
provide positive evidence, including sensitivity to electrophysiological
components such as alpha activity~\cite{ogg2025features}. The unresolved
issue is therefore the strength and transferability of physiological
evidence, rather than its complete absence from the literature.

\subsection{Scope and Research Questions}

Our data preparation spans 14 corpora covering several EEG paradigms and
clinical applications. The experiments reported here have a narrower scope:
we compare cross-subject BCI2A decoding and montage sensitivity, and inspect
REVE geometry on BCI2A, FACED, and PhysioNet motor imagery. We ask whether
class information is accessible in frozen features, how readout and montage
choices affect decoding, and whether latent organization supports a
physiological interpretation beyond subject-associated variation.

The contribution is an empirical audit with explicit limits on these
inferences. We separate evaluation protocols from results, compare against
EEGNet, and combine predictive scores with descriptive diagnostics.

We also developed a hierarchical encoder with a training objective based
on LeJEPA (\cref{sec:proposed-encoder}). However, within the time available
for this internship, we were unable to bring this training to convergence.
No downstream results are therefore reported for this architecture.
This limitation leaves its effectiveness unresolved and does not establish
that LeJEPA is unsuitable for EEG.

REVE cosine diagnostics additionally cover FACED~\cite{chen2023faced}

\subsection{Models, Splits, and Reporting}

The eight foundation models are evaluated with pretrained backbone weights.
EEGNet is trained from scratch as a supervised baseline. The pooled SVM
uses seven training and two test subjects; the token-preserving head and
montage ablation use seven training, one validation, and one test subject.
These distinct protocols answer related questions but do not form a
controlled head-only ablation. Each decoding score describes one run,
without repeated-split uncertainty estimates.

Balanced accuracy is the mean recall across classes and is the primary
comparison metric; its four-class chance reference is 25\%. Accuracy and
macro-F1 provide complementary summaries. Numerical differences are
reported descriptively, without significance claims. Diagnostic bootstrap
intervals concern subjects, while PCA dispersions concern overlapping
folds; neither supplies uncertainty for the separate decoding results.

\subsection{Average-Pooled Linear SVM}
\label{sec:svm-method}

For each frozen feature extractor, all intermediate token dimensions are
averaged while preserving the final feature dimension. Thus, a tensor
$H\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C\times L\times D}$ is reduced to
\begin{equation}
    \bar h_b=\frac{1}{CL}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{l=1}^{L}H_{bcl:}
    \in\mathbb{R}^{D}.
\end{equation}
Here $B$ is the batch size, $C$ the channel count, $L$ the number of
temporal tokens, and $D$ the feature width. Pooling removes explicit
channel and temporal indexing. A linear support
vector machine (SVM) is fitted after feature-wise standardization, with
the mean and standard deviation estimated from training examples only.
The regularization parameter is fixed at 1, with inverse-frequency class
weights. No supervised adaptation of the feature extractor or input
channel projections is performed in this protocol.

All runs use mapped channels and a subject-disjoint split with seven
training subjects and two test subjects (random seed 100). No validation
set or hyperparameter search is used for this comparison. The reported
metrics summarize test epochs pooled across the two held-out subjects;
they are not subject-level means or leave-one-subject-out estimates.

\subsection{Common Token-Preserving Readout}
\label{sec:head-method}

\paragraph{Common readout.}
All pretrained models use the same readout design, adapted to each backbone's output
shape, with separately learned head parameters. Pretrained backbone weights
remain fixed throughout supervised training. Dropout and other
training-dependent operations within the backbone remain in evaluation
mode.

Let $H_b=(h_{b1},\ldots,h_{bS})$ be the latent tokens of an example, with
$h_{bs}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$. Channel, patch, or summary-token axes are combined
into the index $s$, without averaging their representations. A shared
affine projection reduces each token to $q=32$ features, followed by
concatenation and an affine classifier with $K$ outputs:
\begin{align}
    u_{bs}&=P\,\operatorname{Dropout}_{0.2}(h_{bs})+a,\\
    \ell_b&=W(u_{b1}\Vert\cdots\Vert u_{bS})+b,
    \label{eq:common-probe}
\end{align}
Here $\Vert$ denotes concatenation, $P\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times D}$, and
$W\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times32S}$. The row-wise weight norms of these two
maps are constrained to at most 1 and 0.25, respectively.

There is no nonlinear activation between the two maps. With dropout
disabled at evaluation, the logits are therefore affine in the flattened
latent features. This is a factorized linear probe: all token positions
share the same 32-dimensional feature projection. It is more constrained
than an unrestricted linear classifier on all $SD$ features. A common
head design also does not imply equal capacity: its parameter count is
$32(D+1)+K(32S+1)$ and varies with the backbone's token count and width.

\paragraph{Input adaptation.}
EEGPT and LaBraM additionally use a learned $1\times1$ channel convolution
before the backbone. Freezing the backbone leaves this adapter trainable,
and the optimizer updates it together with the head. Their protocol is thus frozen-backbone probing with input
adaptation, rather than probing a completely fixed feature extractor.
This distinction must accompany the results, since changes to the adapter
can change the representations even though pretrained weights remain fixed.

\paragraph{Training and evaluation.}
Subjects are assigned to disjoint training, validation, and test sets using
target proportions of 80\%, 10\%, and 10\%, with at least one subject in
each held-out set. For BCI2A this gives seven training subjects, one
validation subject, and one test subject. A random seed of 100 is used for
both the subject split and training randomization.
This is a single subject-disjoint split, not leave-one-subject-out
cross-validation.

For the pretrained models, training uses cross-entropy without label
smoothing, AdamW with learning rate $4\times10^{-4}$ and weight decay 0.01,
batches of 64, and gradient-norm clipping at 1. Training lasts at most
50 epochs, with early stopping after ten epochs without improvement in
validation balanced accuracy. The best validation checkpoint is restored
before test evaluation. Accuracy, balanced accuracy (mean class recall),
and macro-F1 are recorded; balanced accuracy is the selection criterion.

EEGNet is a separate supervised baseline trained without pretrained
weights. Its backbone is optimized together with its classifier, so its
results must be distinguished from those obtained with frozen pretrained
representations. Its training uses AdamW with learning rate $10^{-3}$,
weight decay $10^{-4}$, and dropout 0.25, with the same subject split and
validation-based selection criterion.

\subsection{Montage Transformation}
\label{sec:montage-method}

The montage comparison assesses the effect of replacing the native channel
layout with a fixed target layout. Here, \emph{raw} denotes native channels
from the preprocessed dataset, not unprocessed EEG. The mapped condition
uses the nearest-source rule illustrated in \cref{fig:channel-mapping}.
For target electrode $i$, the closest source electrode $j^*(i)$ supplies
the complete channel signal if its distance is at most $r$; otherwise the
target channel is filled with zeros.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ablation_mapped_vs_raw/channelmapping.pdf}
    \caption{Channel-mapping rule for the montage ablation. Each fixed target
    electrode independently selects the closest source within a distance
    threshold; targets without an eligible source are zero-filled. The
    symbols $z$ and $z'$ denote channel signals in this schematic, not the
    latent tokens used elsewhere in the report. This figure describes the
    transformation and contains no downstream performance comparison.}
    \label{fig:channel-mapping}
\end{figure*}

This operation copies signals rather than interpolating several electrodes.
Different target locations can select the same source, and sources that
are never selected are omitted. Mapping therefore changes spatial sampling
and may introduce duplicate or missing channels. Its effect is not limited
to channel ordering, and improved performance would not by itself demonstrate
montage invariance or recovery of unobserved activity.

We evaluate the frozen CBraMod and EEGMamba backbones with the common
head, alongside EEGNet trained end to end. Each condition uses
seven training subjects, one validation subject, and one test subject.
The intended control keeps the subject split, underlying windows, labels,
checkpoint, and training settings fixed within each model. Mapping can
nevertheless change the readout size through the channel count, so this
comparison measures the effect of the complete montage-processing pipeline.

\subsection{Latent-Space Diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostic-method}

All descriptive figures use REVE with mapped channels. They are not
cross-model evidence. Pooling and projection choices differ by analysis
and are specified below so that their conclusions are not conflated.

\paragraph{t-SNE.}
The t-SNE analysis~\cite{vandermaaten2008tsne} uses 1,800 BCI2A epochs from
REVE, sampled without replacement at 200 epochs per subject across all nine
subjects. Sampling is balanced by subject, not explicitly by class. Token
features are averaged over the channel and temporal axes, yielding one
512-dimensional vector per epoch. Features are standardized across the
visualization sample, reduced to 50 principal components, and embedded
with perplexity 30, PCA initialization, and 1,000 optimization iterations
(random seed 100).

\paragraph{Within-subject cosine similarity.}
To examine class-related angular structure while conditioning on subject,
we compare distinct pairs of epochs from the same participant. REVE tokens
are averaged over all axes except batch and feature dimension, then
$\ell_2$-normalized without centering. For pooled features $h_a,h_b$, the
similarity is $s_{ab}=h_a^\top h_b/(\|h_a\|_2\|h_b\|_2)$. The retained
samples contain 2,997 epochs from nine BCI2A subjects, 2,952 from 123 FACED
subjects, and 2,943 from 109 PHYSIONETMI subjects.

For subject $u$, define
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_u=\overline{s}_{u,\mathrm{same}}
             -\overline{s}_{u,\mathrm{different}},
    \qquad \overline{\Delta}=\frac{1}{U}\sum_{u=1}^{U}\Delta_u.
    \label{eq:cosine-gap}
\end{equation}
$U$ is the number of retained subjects. Each subject's means use all
eligible pairs among their retained epochs.
The reported 95\% percentile bootstrap interval resamples subjects
5,000 times, rather than treating the many dependent pairs as independent
observations. Histograms use equal numbers of same-class and different-class
pairs within each subject, with a common per-subject cap derived from a
50,000-pair budget per condition. Subjects with fewer eligible pairs
contribute fewer histogram samples.

\paragraph{Training-only PCA.}
PCA retains all REVE tokens as a single flattened vector, whereas t-SNE
and cosine similarity use pooled features. The BCI2A analysis uses nine outer folds, each holding
out one test subject and the next subject in cyclic order for validation.
Ten principal components are fitted to centered, unstandardized features
from the remaining seven subjects. The 512-epoch training budget allocates
73 epochs per subject, giving 511 sampled training epochs per fold.
Neither validation nor test examples enter the PCA fit.

The displayed fold reserves A01 for testing and shows validation subject
A02. RGB colors encode the first three component scores after scaling each
component by its training-set 1st and 99th percentiles and clipping to
$[0,1]$. Colors are therefore an unsupervised summary of three latent
coordinates, not predicted labels, physiological categories, or a measure
of confidence. The color transform is also fitted without validation or
test data.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
These opposite effects argue against a universal benefit of channel
mapping. Retaining native observations may benefit the pretrained models,
whereas a fixed layout may help the supervised baseline; these mechanisms
remain hypotheses. Mapping also omits, duplicates, or zero-fills channels,
and the selected epochs differ between conditions. The results therefore
do not isolate geometry from information loss, model capacity, or
optimization effects. The distance threshold, target montage, coverage
statistics, and repeated subject-level comparisons are still needed for a
more complete account of these differences.

In \cref{fig:tsne-subject-class}, the left panel contains recognizable
subject-enriched regions, particularly for A02, A05, A07, and A09, alongside
substantial overlap elsewhere. Coloring the same coordinates by task class
reveals no comparably distinct class regions. Subject membership is thus

This observation motivates controlling subject identity in subsequent
analyses and keeping subjects separate during downstream evaluation.

The plot does not quantify how much subject or class information is present
in the full representation. Averaging tokens can discard task information,
and a nonlinear two-dimensional embedding can obscure class separation
available in other directions. Nor can subject-associated structure
distinguish physiology from session or acquisition effects. Consequently,
these clusters provide descriptive evidence of subject-related organization,
not proof of artifact dependence or failed cross-subject decoding.

The distributions overlap strongly (\cref{fig:cosine-within-subject}).
For BCI2A, the mean subject gap is about $0.0001$; its displayed
interval is $[0.0000,0.0001]$. The lower endpoint is rounded, so its printed
value is insufficient to decide whether the unrounded interval excludes
zero. FACED and PHYSIONETMI have larger positive gaps,
$0.0021\;[0.0011,0.0032]$ and $0.0010\;[0.0006,0.0015]$, respectively.
These positive intervals support a small average class association in
angular similarity within the sampled subjects, not a decoding accuracy.

Absolute similarities are high for both pair types, with displayed means
between approximately 0.957 and 0.987. This indicates strongly aligned
pooled vectors relative to the origin. A shared mean component can produce
such alignment because features were not centered. High cosine similarity
therefore does not establish representation collapse, and a small mean
gap does not exclude informative low-variance directions. The subject-wise
summary also differs from the histogram mean difference: for example,
FACED's pair-weighted means differ by 0.0036, while its equally weighted
subject gap is 0.0021.

\subsection{PCA across Subjects and Epoch-Level Signal Inspection}
\label{sec:pca}

\paragraph{Variance and stability.}
The cumulative top-ten training explained variance is $21.20\%\pm0.31\%$ across folds. Subspace stability is assessed
by refitting PCA on five random 80\% subsets of each fold's training
sample. For reference and refitted orthonormal loading matrices $V$ and
$\widetilde V$, the score is
$10^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{10}\sigma_k^2(V\widetilde V^\top)$, the mean squared
cosine of their principal angles. The mean fold score is
$0.909\pm0.014$. These dispersions are fold standard deviations, not
confidence intervals; folds share much of their training data.

The same components capture $14.08\%\pm2.37\%$ of held-out test energy,
measured relative to the \emph{training} mean. This is not variance about
the test subject's own mean and can include a between-subject mean shift.
The lower capture than on training data is compatible with a shift in
feature structure, but also with fitting PCA to maximize training variance.
The results indicate a reasonably stable leading subspace under training
subsampling while leaving most feature variation outside its ten dimensions.
They do not measure class discrimination.

\paragraph{Class overlap and signal context.}
In the displayed fold, PC1 and PC2 account for 5.1\% and 3.1\% of training
variance. The four validation classes overlap extensively in this plane
(\cref{fig:pca-scatter}), and their average RGB colors are similar
(\cref{fig:pca-rgb}). Together, these observations show little visual class
separation in the leading coordinates for A02. Since the first two
components explain only about 8.2\% of training variance and PCA ignores
labels, this does not rule out separation in later components or in the
full token representation.

\Cref{fig:pca-epochs} places the latent colors alongside eight reproducibly
sampled epochs. Background colors vary between examples, including examples
of the same class; the seventh displayed epoch contains particularly large
transients on the plotted channel. This juxtaposition motivates testing
associations with signal amplitude or artifacts, but it does not establish
such an association. The embedding uses all channels, the trace shows only
one, and neither artifact labels nor quantitative signal--latent correlations
are included. Calling these transients ocular or muscular artifacts would
therefore exceed the available evidence.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Predictive accuracy and physiological evidence.}
These experiments distinguish successful prediction from evidence that a
representation captures task-relevant physiological mechanisms. Neither
classification accuracy nor a visually structured latent space identifies
the source of the predictive signal. The present results do not establish
that the pretrained features encode transferable physiological markers,
independently of participant characteristics, acquisition conditions, or
artifacts. This is a limitation of the evidence, rather than proof that
such markers are absent from the representations.

Physiological evidence in the broader literature is heterogeneous.
For example, relevance analyses identify task-appropriate brain
regions, although stable attribution maps can coexist with degraded
predictions under perturbation~\cite{sirca2026beyond}. Other work separates
subject-related information from established physiological markers through
targeted controls~\cite{lin2026identity}. A separate study demonstrates representation sensitivity to alpha activity
and individual electrophysiological variation~\cite{ogg2025features}.
Such findings preclude a blanket claim that foundation models have learned
no physiological features. They do not, however, establish that the same
mechanism supports reliable decoding across subjects and recording conditions.

\paragraph{Subject structure and transfer.}
The REVE t-SNE display contains more apparent subject organization than
class organization. Together with the weak pooled-SVM results, this raises
a concrete concern: learning may retain subject-dependent cues that do not
transfer reliably to new participants. These cues could include artifacts,
but also genuine individual physiology or session effects. The plot alone
cannot identify their origin, quantify their dominance in the full space,
or extend this finding to every evaluated model. Independent diagnostic
work nevertheless supports treating subject identity as a serious potential
shortcut in foundation-model evaluation~\cite{lin2026identity}.

Subject-disjoint evaluation is necessary for testing transfer, but one
held-out participant cannot characterize a population. Repeated splits or
leave-one-subject-out evaluation, followed by cross-session and cross-dataset
testing, would strengthen the conclusions. Subject-identity decoding and
comparisons with artifact annotations would help determine whether an
improvement reflects task information or a persistent nuisance variable.
The observed montage sensitivity further shows that transfer depends on
how recordings are presented to the model.

\paragraph{A compact supervised baseline.}
The evaluated EEGNet baseline has approximately 3,000 parameters, yet it
outperforms every frozen foundation model in the mapped common-head
comparison. Its balanced accuracy of 76.77\% exceeds the best pretrained
result, REVE at 65.21\%, by 11.56 points. EEGNet also leads the montage
ablation in both mapped and raw conditions. Thus, it is consistently the
stronger baseline within the reported comparable experiments, despite its
small size. This supports retaining compact, task-specific models as
serious comparators~\cite{lawhern2018eegnet}.

This advantage is conditional on the evaluated training regimes: EEGNet
learns all its features from task labels, whereas the foundation backbones
remain frozen. Their optimization settings also differ. The findings
therefore do not establish that EEGNet always exceeds foundation models
across datasets, tasks, or full fine-tuning protocols. They show that model
scale and pretraining alone are insufficient to guarantee better decoding
in this BCI2A evaluation.

\paragraph{Pooling, dimensionality, and interpretability.}
Average-pooled features yield only 26.80--33.31\% balanced accuracy with a
linear SVM, compared with a 25\% chance reference. They consequently show
weak transferable linear separation under this protocol. Pooling can
remove contrasts between channels or time points, however, so these
results cannot establish inseparability of all latent features. Prior
work similarly identifies pooling as a limitation of frozen-feature
assessment~\cite{sirca2026beyond}. The higher token-preserving scores motivate
a matched pooling ablation rather than a claim that nonlinear classification
alone resolves the problem.

High-dimensional representations also complicate interpretation. Retaining
more tokens increases the downstream parameter count and can improve the
fit to incidental correlations when subjects are few. Some representation
similarity measures have specific high-dimensional failure modes: for
example, CCA-type statistics become uninformative when feature dimension
exceeds the number of observations~\cite{kornblith2019similarity}. This does
not imply that high dimensionality automatically inflates properly held-out
accuracy, nor that the reported scores are artifacts. It motivates matched
probe capacities, training-only dimensionality reduction, and shuffled-label
or random-feature controls that respect the subject structure.

Accuracy should therefore be accompanied by evidence about what the model
uses: perturbations of selected frequency bands and regions, sensitivity
to physiological markers, robustness to artifact removal, and replication
across subjects. The strongly aligned uncentered cosine vectors and the
small leading-component class contrast are useful diagnostics, but neither
is a direct measurement of physiological meaning. A favorable metric must
be interpreted together with its feature transformation, effective capacity,
and sampling unit.

\paragraph{Learning invariances with LeJEPA.}
LeJEPA offers a way to align representations of related views while
regularizing their distribution. More precisely, it is a non-contrastive
joint-embedding method: its anti-collapse mechanism uses SIGReg rather
than explicit negative pairs~\cite{balestriero2025lejepa}. Contrastive
self-supervision has separately shown strong results on time-series
benchmarks, including multivariate data, as illustrated by
TS2Vec~\cite{yue2022ts2vec}. LuMamba further evaluates LeJEPA on EEG and reports stronger transfer
with a combined reconstruction and alignment objective than with either
alone~\cite{broustail2026lumamba}. These results motivate hybrid as well as
pure latent objectives; they do not establish physiological specificity
or invariance to artifacts.

For an EEG application, a central research objective is to construct views
that preserve the neural phenomenon of interest while varying nuisance
components. Candidate augmentations include independently varying
physiologically plausible ocular or muscle contamination, bounded sensor
gain changes, and spatially constrained channel masking. Where paired
contaminated and artifact-reduced signals are available, they could provide
an additional invariance test. These are proposed experiments, not outcomes
established by the present benchmark.

The transformations must be validated for the task: aggressive filtering,
amplitude changes, or temporal warping may remove neural information along
with artifacts. Augmentations should vary nuisance content independently
between views; an artifact preserved in both views can itself satisfy the
alignment objective. Equally, suppressing all subject information is not
an appropriate universal target, since some individual physiological
variation may be relevant. Useful invariance must be demonstrated through
artifact robustness together with preserved marker sensitivity and
held-out task performance.

\paragraph{Limitations and priorities for validation.}
The number of prepared corpora exceeds the coverage of the reported
experiments. Eight-model decoding results on one task and diagnostic
figures from one encoder cannot establish universal properties of EEG
foundation learning. External models also differ in pretraining data,
architecture, representation size, and input adaptation, so this audit
cannot causally rank their pretraining objectives. Single-run scores and
one held-out test subject in the common-head comparison limit population-level
claims. The two EEGNet mapped runs are distinct observations, not a
replication estimate.

A stronger evaluation should cross readout type and pooling under identical
subject splits, control trainable adapters and classifier capacity, and
compare frozen, fine-tuned, and randomly initialized encoders. Multiple
seeds and held-out subjects should accompany subject-level uncertainty
estimates. Artifact annotations, physiological marker tests, and
cross-session or cross-corpus transfer would then address whether gains
reflect useful invariances. Incomplete reporting of dataset versions, exact split identities,
preprocessing choices, montage specifications, and potential pretraining
overlap further limits reproducibility and attribution of performance
differences.

\balance
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The central challenge for EEG foundation models is to learn latent
representations that preserve relevant multivariate dynamics while
remaining useful across subjects and recording conditions. In the present
BCI2A experiments, compact supervised EEGNet outperforms all frozen
foundation backbones under the compared protocols. Average-pooled SVM
features show weak transferable class discrimination, and montage changes
have model-dependent effects. REVE diagnostics reveal subject-associated
organization without identifying its physiological or artifactual origin.
These observations qualify the evidence for general-purpose representations;
they do not establish their impossibility.

No learning objective can be selected as universally best from these data.
Signal reconstruction retains detailed observations but need not prioritize
physiological mechanisms. Latent alignment can encourage invariance while
also preserving nuisance factors shared between views. Joint spatial and
temporal modeling, meaningful prediction targets, and carefully validated
augmentations should therefore be studied together. LeJEPA and hybrid
reconstruction--alignment objectives are relevant candidates, provided
that the intended invariances preserve task-related neural information.
The additional encoder specification in \cref{sec:proposed-encoder} is a
proposal for such experiments, not an empirically supported solution.

The breadth of the prepared EEG corpora makes evaluation across clinical,
affective, motor, sleep, and speech-related settings possible, but that
broader validation remains to be reported. Progress should be judged by
reproducible cross-subject and cross-dataset transfer, competitive compact
baselines, and direct evidence about the physiological content of latent
features. Model size, low reconstruction error, or favorable geometric
metrics alone are insufficient criteria for useful foundation learning.

\section{Proposed Encoder and Joint-Embedding Training}
\label{sec:proposed-encoder}

This appendix specifies an additional hierarchical EEG encoder and a
candidate training procedure. None of the quantitative results in
\cref{sec:results} evaluates this architecture. The design is retained to
make the proposed research direction precise; its components, augmentations,
and objective require controlled ablation before any performance or
physiological claim can be made. It is distinct from the eight pretrained
models compared in the main text.

The encoder combines electrode-wise tokenization, spatial mixing, and
bidirectional temporal processing. It returns channel-resolved tokens
and a global representation. The proposed global objective follows
LeJEPA-style view agreement and distributional regularization without
waveform reconstruction. The specification below defines a patch-based
configuration, rather than a validated optimal architecture.

Let $X\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C\times T}$ denote a batch of EEG windows, where
$B$ is the batch size, $C$ the number of channels in a fixed canonical order,
and $T$ the number of time samples. Electrode coordinates are denoted by
$P=(p_1,\ldots,p_C)^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times3}$.
A presence code $a_{bc}\in\{0,1,2\}$ accompanies each channel; code zero marks
an absent channel, and both nonzero codes are treated as available by the
attention masks. We write $m_{bc}=\mathbb{1}[a_{bc}\ne0]$ and assume that each
window contains at least one available channel. Hereafter, $\Vert$ denotes
concatenation, $\odot$ element-wise multiplication, and $\RN$ and $\LN$
denote root-mean-square normalization and layer normalization, respectively.
Features are column vectors, and linear maps act on their last dimension.
The presence input is labeled $p$ in the figures and $a$ in the text.

\subsection{Model Pipeline Overview}
\label{sec:model-overview}

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{model_pipeline/model_pipeline.pdf}
    \caption{Model architecture. A patch stem feeds four encoder stages with
    progressively shorter temporal sequences and larger feature dimensions.
    Geodesic distances between electrode coordinates are quantized into
    16 bins and mapped to learned attention biases in the last two stages.
    The bottleneck returns token embeddings $z$ and a global embedding
    $z_{\mathrm{glob}}$ obtained with four learned queries. The input has shape
    $B\times C\times T$; stage lengths are
    $N_i=\lfloor T/(20\cdot2^{i-1})\rfloor$ for $i=1,\ldots,4$.
    Feature widths correspond to the proposed architecture.}
    \label{fig:model-pipeline}
\end{figure*}

\Cref{fig:model-pipeline} summarizes the mapping
\begin{equation}
    (Z,g)=f_\theta(X,a;P),
    \label{eq:model-mapping}
\end{equation}
where $Z\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C\times N_4\times D_z}$ contains the token
embeddings and $g\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times D_z}$ contains the global embeddings.
These correspond to the outputs $z$ and $z_{\mathrm{glob}}$ in the figures.
The channel axis is preserved throughout the encoder; only temporal
resolution and feature dimension change. The input length $T$ is variable;
the encoder uses $D_z=384$. At a sampling rate of 200\,Hz,
an input window spans $T/200$ seconds.

\paragraph{Signal tokenization.}
The stem first applies a signed logarithmic compression,
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde X=\operatorname{sign}(X)\odot\log(1+|X|).
    \label{eq:signed-log}
\end{equation}
Each channel is processed independently by the same two temporal convolutional
layers. Both have kernel size nine and are followed by eight-group GroupNorm
and SiLU. The first convolution maps one input feature to $d_s=32$ features;
the second preserves this width and uses eight convolution groups.
Padding preserves the time dimension. The resulting features are partitioned
into non-overlapping patches of $p=20$ samples. With
$N_1=\lfloor T/p\rfloor$, the flattened patch vector
$r_{bcn}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd_s}$ is embedded as
\begin{equation}
    H^{(0)}_{bcn}
    =W_{\mathrm{stem}}\LN(r_{bcn})+e_c+e_{a_{bc}},
    \label{eq:patch-embedding}
\end{equation}
where $W_{\mathrm{stem}}\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times640}$, $e_c$ is a learned
channel embedding, and $e_{a_{bc}}$ is a learned presence embedding.
Any incomplete final patch is discarded.

\paragraph{Hierarchical encoding.}
The four stages use feature widths
$(D_1,D_2,D_3,D_4)=(128,192,256,384)$ and block counts
$(L_1,L_2,L_3,L_4)=(2,2,3,3)$. Between stages, adjacent tokens are concatenated,
normalized, and projected:
\begin{align}
    r^{(i)}_{bcn}
      &=H^{(i)}_{bc,2n-1}\Vert H^{(i)}_{bc,2n},\\
    H^{(i+1,\mathrm{in})}_{bcn}
      &=W_{\downarrow}^{(i)}\RN(r^{(i)}_{bcn}),
    \label{eq:downsampling}
\end{align}
where $W_{\downarrow}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{i+1}\times2D_i}$ and
$n=1,\ldots,\lfloor N_i/2\rfloor$. An unpaired final token is discarded.
Thus, for patch size $p=20$, the temporal lengths satisfy
\begin{equation}
    N_i=\left\lfloor\frac{T}{p\,2^{i-1}}\right\rfloor,
    \qquad i=1,\ldots,4.
    \label{eq:stage-lengths}
\end{equation}
In particular, $N_4=\lfloor T/160\rfloor$; we require $T\geq160$ so that
at least one temporal token reaches the bottleneck.

\paragraph{Token bottleneck and global readout.}
The final hidden tensor is normalized and optionally projected:
\begin{equation}
    Z_{bcn}=W_z\RN(H^{(4)}_{bcn}).
    \label{eq:token-bottleneck}
\end{equation}
Here $W_z$ is the identity because $D_z=D_4$; a learned projection is used
when these dimensions differ. To obtain $g_b$, the $C N_4$ tokens are
flattened into a sequence $(z_{bj})_j$. For each of $A=8$ attention heads,
linear maps produce keys and values of width $d_z=D_z/A=48$:
\begin{equation}
    k_{bhj}=\RN(W_h^Kz_{bj}),\qquad
    v_{bhj}=W_h^Vz_{bj}.
\end{equation}
Each head has $Q=4$ learned queries $q_{hq}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_z}$. A token
inherits the availability of its channel, denoted by $m_{bj}$. The pooled
head outputs are
\begin{align}
    \alpha_{bhqj}
      &=\frac{m_{bj}\exp(q_{hq}^{\top}k_{bhj}/\sqrt{d_z})}
      {\sum_r m_{br}\exp(q_{hq}^{\top}k_{bhr}/\sqrt{d_z})},\\
    o_{bhq}&=\sum_j\alpha_{bhqj}v_{bhj}.
    \label{eq:global-attention}
\end{align}
Concatenating the heads for each query and then the queries gives
$o_b\in\mathbb{R}^{QD_z}$. The global representation is
\begin{equation}
    g_b=W_{\mathrm{out}}W_{\mathrm{merge}}\RN(o_b),
    \label{eq:global-embedding}
\end{equation}
with $W_{\mathrm{merge}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_z\times QD_z}$ and
$W_{\mathrm{out}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_z\times D_z}$. Thus, the global readout
aggregates both channel and temporal information while excluding unavailable
channels as attention keys. Training uses $g_b$ directly; the token output
$Z$ remains available for downstream analysis.

\subsection{Encoder Architecture}
\label{sec:encoder}

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{encoder_stage/encoder_stage.pdf}
    \caption{Encoder block and its constituent operations. Four pre-normalized
    residual branches perform spatial mixing, bidirectional Mamba2 processing,
    local temporal convolution, and a gated feed-forward transformation.
    Spatial attention is added to the convolutional spatial branch in stages
    three and four. $\widetilde h$ denotes a branch input after its outer
    normalization; $M(p)$ in the diagram is the additive channel-availability
    mask associated with the presence codes $a$ in the text.}
    \label{fig:encoder-block}
\end{figure*}

Every block preserves its input shape $B\times C\times N\times D$.
As shown in \cref{fig:encoder-block}, its four residual updates take the form
\begin{equation}
    U_r=U_{r-1}+\operatorname{DP}_{\rho}
    \bigl(\gamma_r\odot F_r(\RN_r(U_{r-1}))\bigr),
    \label{eq:residual-block}
\end{equation}
for $r=1,\ldots,4$, with $F_r$ denoting spatial mixing, temporal Mamba2,
Conformer convolution, and SwiGLU, respectively. Each branch has its own
normalization parameters and feature-wise LayerScale vector
$\gamma_r\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$, initialized to $10^{-4}$.
During training, DropPath multiplies a branch by
$\xi/(1-\rho)$, with $\xi\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1-\rho)$ independently
sampled per batch example and branch. The rate $\rho$ increases linearly
from zero to 0.15 over the ten blocks. Ordinary dropout is zero in the
reference architecture.

\paragraph{Convolutional spatial mixing.}
Let $U$ denote the input to the spatial module, already normalized by the
outer residual branch. SpatialConv applies an additional RMSNorm and masks
unavailable input channels:
\begin{equation}
    G_{bcnd}=m_{bc}\,[\RN(U_{bcn})]_d.
\end{equation}
At every temporal and feature index, two $1\times1$ convolutions mix the
channel axis:
\begin{equation}
    S^{\mathrm{conv}}_{b,:,n,d}
    =W_{c,2}\SiLU(W_{c,1}G_{b,:,n,d}),
    \label{eq:spatial-conv}
\end{equation}
where $W_{c,1},W_{c,2}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times C}$.
This branch is present in all stages and uses a fixed channel ordering.

\paragraph{Montage-dependent attention bias.}
Coordinates $p_i$ are taken from the standard 10--20 montage for the mapped
channel names. We normalize them and compute angular distances:
\begin{align}
    u_i&=\frac{p_i}{\max(\|p_i\|_2,\varepsilon)},\\
    d_{ij}&=\arccos\!\left(\operatorname{clip}(u_i^\top u_j,-1,1)\right).
\end{align}
The distances are discretized into $K_d=16$ bins,
\begin{equation}
    \beta_{ij}=\operatorname{round}\!\left(
      \frac{(K_d-1)d_{ij}}{\max(\max_{r,s}d_{rs},\varepsilon)}
    \right),
    \label{eq:distance-bins}
\end{equation}
where the denominator is clamped from below by
$\varepsilon=10^{-8}$. The resulting integer indices remain fixed during training.
A learned table $E\in\mathbb{R}^{A\times K_d}$, initialized to zero,
defines the head-specific bias $b_{hij}=E_{h,\beta_{ij}}$.
This table is shared across the attention blocks of the encoder.

\paragraph{Statistics-conditioned spatial attention.}
Stages three and four add a beamformer-style attention branch to
\cref{eq:spatial-conv}. For each channel, queries and keys are conditioned on
statistics over the temporal tokens:
\begin{align}
    \mu_{bc}&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}U_{bcn},\\
    v_{bc}&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(U_{bcn}-\mu_{bc})^{\odot2},\\
    \ell_{bc}&=\log\bigl(\max(v_{bc},\varepsilon)\bigr),\\
    s_{bc}&=\LN(\mu_{bc}\Vert\ell_{bc}).
    \label{eq:channel-statistics}
\end{align}
The maximum and logarithm are element-wise, with $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$.
Each of the eight heads has feature width $d_h=D/A$ and computes
\begin{align}
    q_{bhi}&=\LN(W_h^Qs_{bi}),\\
    k_{bhj}&=\LN(W_h^Ks_{bj}).
\end{align}
The spatial weights are
\begin{align}
    e_{bhij}&=q_{bhi}^{\top}k_{bhj}/\sqrt{d_h}+b_{hij},\\
    A_{bhij}&=\frac{m_{bj}\exp(e_{bhij})}
                      {\sum_r m_{br}\exp(e_{bhir})}.
    \label{eq:spatial-attention}
\end{align}
Values retain the temporal axis: $v_{bhjn}=W_h^VU_{bjn}$.
For each time index, the attention output is
\begin{equation}
    S^{\mathrm{attn}}_{bin}
      =W_O\!\left(\mathop{\Vert}_{h=1}^{A}
        \sum_j A_{bhij}v_{bhjn}\right).
\end{equation}
A single spatial weight matrix per window and head is therefore reused
across all temporal tokens. The spatial residual branch receives
$S^{\mathrm{conv}}$ in stages one and two and
$S^{\mathrm{conv}}+S^{\mathrm{attn}}$ in stages three and four.
The geometric bias informs attention about relative electrode locations;
the channel embeddings and convolutional mixing nevertheless retain a fixed
channel indexing, so this construction does not imply arbitrary-montage
or channel-permutation invariance.

\paragraph{Bidirectional temporal state-space processing.}
Temporal dependencies are modeled with two independently parameterized
Mamba2 modules~\cite{dao2024mamba2}. After an internal RMSNorm, the tensor
is reshaped to $(BC,N,D)$, treating each electrode as a separate sequence.
Writing $R$ for reversal along the token axis and $\bar U=\RN(U)$, the
bidirectional output is
\begin{equation}
    F_{\mathrm{temp}}(U)
      =\mathcal{M}_{\rightarrow}(\bar U)
       +R\!\left[\mathcal{M}_{\leftarrow}(R[\bar U])\right].
    \label{eq:bimamba}
\end{equation}
Reversing the backward output restores the original token alignment before
summation. Each Mamba2 module uses state dimension 128, expansion factor two,
local convolution width four, and head dimension 64. This module mixes
information across time within each electrode; interactions between
electrodes are provided by the spatial branch.

For completeness, the selective state-space core of a single Mamba2 head
can be expressed in recurrent form as
\begin{align}
    S_n&=\eta_n S_{n-1}+b_n v_n^\top,\\
    o_n&=S_n^\top c_n+d\,v_n,
    \label{eq:mamba-state}
\end{align}
where $S_n\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times64}$, $v_n\in\mathbb{R}^{64}$,
and $b_n,c_n\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$. The scalar transition $\eta_n$ and the
input and readout vectors depend on the input; $b_n$ includes the input
scaling from discretization, and $d$ is a learned direct-path coefficient.
This scalar-identity transition is the structured state-space component of
Mamba2~\cite{dao2024mamba2}. The complete module also includes its input
projections, local convolution, gating, normalization, and output projection.
\Cref{eq:bimamba} combines two such complete modules in opposite directions.

\paragraph{Local temporal convolution and gated feed-forward mapping.}
The Conformer branch supplements the state-space module with a local
convolution. A linear map expands $U$ from $D$ to $4D$ features and splits
it into $a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}$. The gated features are
$v=a\odot\sigma(b)$, where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid. A depthwise temporal
convolution of kernel size 15, with padding that preserves $N$, is followed
by single-group GroupNorm, SiLU, and a projection back to $D$:
\begin{equation}
    F_{\mathrm{conv}}(U)
      =W_2\SiLU\!\left(\operatorname{GN}
        (\operatorname{DWConv}_{15}(v))\right).
\end{equation}
Finally, the feature-wise SwiGLU branch is
\begin{equation}
    F_{\mathrm{ffn}}(U)=W_3
      \bigl(\SiLU(W_1U)\odot W_2U\bigr).
\end{equation}
Its hidden width is $\lfloor8D/3\rfloor$ rounded upward to the nearest
multiple of 64. All four operations are wrapped by the residual update
in \cref{eq:residual-block}.

\subsection{Training Pipeline Overview}
\label{sec:training}

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{training_pipeline/training_pipeline.pdf}
    \caption{Multiview LeJEPA training. Two global and six local views are
    encoded by the same shared model. All view embeddings are aligned
    with the mean of the global embeddings of their source window. SIGReg
    is evaluated across the batch separately for each view and then averaged.
    The weighted loss updates the complete encoder and global readout.
    The global center remains differentiable.}
    \label{fig:training-pipeline}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Multiview joint-embedding learning.}
LeJEPA combines a joint-embedding agreement objective with Sketched
Isotropic Gaussian Regularization~\cite{balestriero2025lejepa}, abbreviated SIGReg.
Our EEG adaptation applies both terms directly to the global readout
in \cref{eq:global-embedding}. It uses a single shared encoder, with no
separate teacher, stop-gradient target, predictor network, or additional
projection head. The training objective does not reconstruct EEG signals.

For each source window $X_b$, we sample $V_g=2$ global views
and $V_\ell=6$ local views. We denote their union by
$x_b^{(v)}=\mathcal{T}_v(X_b)$, $v=1,\ldots,V$, where $V=V_g+V_\ell=8$ and
the first $V_g$ indices designate the global views.
The candidate augmentations include additive noise, global and
channel-wise amplitude scaling, temporal shifts, and temporal, frequency,
and spatial masking. These transformations have not been validated as preserving physiological
markers or removing artifacts. The same presence codes $a_b$ accompany
all views of a window; spatial masking is an augmentation of observed
signals rather than a change to the recorded channel-availability metadata. Each view is passed through the same model:
\begin{equation}
    y_b^{(v)}=g_\theta(x_b^{(v)},a_b;P)
      \in\mathbb{R}^{D_z}.
\end{equation}
Here $g_\theta$ denotes the global component of $f_\theta$.
Stacking these outputs yields tensors of shape $B\times V_g\times D_z$
and $B\times V_\ell\times D_z$, as shown in \cref{fig:training-pipeline}.

\paragraph{Alignment with the global-view center.}
The target for every view of window $b$ is the mean of its global embeddings,
\begin{equation}
    c_b=\frac{1}{V_g}\sum_{v=1}^{V_g}y_b^{(v)}.
    \label{eq:global-center}
\end{equation}
Both global and local embeddings contribute to the alignment loss,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}
      =\frac{1}{BVD_z}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{v=1}^{V}
         \|y_b^{(v)}-c_b\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:alignment}
\end{equation}
The factor $D_z^{-1}$ normalizes the loss by the feature dimension.
Gradients propagate through both the individual embeddings and the center
$c_b$. Alignment alone admits a constant representation, motivating the
additional distributional constraint.

\paragraph{Sketched isotropic Gaussian regularization.}
At each optimization step, we sample $K=256$ unit directions
$u_k=\omega_k/\|\omega_k\|_2$, with
$\omega_k\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{D_z})$.
For a fixed view $v$, the one-dimensional projections
$s_{bvk}=u_k^\top y_b^{(v)}$ define an empirical characteristic function
\begin{equation}
    \widehat\varphi_{vk}(t)
       =\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\exp(\mathrm{i}t s_{bvk}).
    \label{eq:empirical-cf}
\end{equation}
The target is the characteristic function of a standard normal variable,
$\varphi_0(t)=\exp(-t^2/2)$. Define
\begin{equation}
    e_{vk}(t)=
      |\widehat\varphi_{vk}(t)-\varphi_0(t)|^2\varphi_0(t).
\end{equation}
The per-view regularizer approximates
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}_v=\frac{B}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
       \int_{-5}^{5}e_{vk}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
    \label{eq:sigreg-integral}
\end{equation}
using the trapezoidal rule on $J=17$ equally spaced points
$t_j=-5+10j/(J-1)$, $j=0,\ldots,J-1$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}_v\approx\frac{B\Delta t}{2K}
      \sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=0}^{J-2}
      \bigl[e_{vk}(t_j)+e_{vk}(t_{j+1})\bigr],
    \label{eq:sigreg-discrete}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t=10/(J-1)$.
The complex discrepancy is evaluated through its real and imaginary parts,
\begin{equation}
    |\widehat\varphi-\varphi_0|^2
      =(\Re\widehat\varphi-\varphi_0)^2
        +(\Im\widehat\varphi)^2.
\end{equation}
This operates on the unnormalized embeddings, so the unit-variance target
also constrains their scale. The final regularizer is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SIG}}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\mathcal{R}_v.
    \label{eq:sigreg-views}
\end{equation}
Thus, views are not pooled together as independent samples for SIGReg.
The same random directions are reused across views within a step by seeding
the sampler with the global step index. In distributed training, characteristic-function sums and sample counts are aggregated
across workers; $B$ in \cref{eq:empirical-cf,eq:sigreg-integral,eq:sigreg-discrete}
then denotes the aggregated batch size. The regularizer is evaluated in
float32 when embeddings are produced in reduced precision.

\balance
\paragraph{Objective and optimization.}
The complete objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}=(1-\lambda)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}
                   +\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SIG}},
    \qquad\lambda=0.05.
    \label{eq:total-objective}
\end{equation}
The proposed training uses normalized TUEG windows with mapped channels,
excluding the TUAB and TUAR subsets. The optimizer is AdamW with
initial learning rate $10^{-4}$, momentum coefficients $(0.9,0.95)$,
and weight decay 0.05. Biases,
normalization parameters, embeddings, learned queries, LayerScale vectors,
and the spatial bias table are excluded from weight decay. The total gradient norm is clipped at one.
A cosine learning-rate schedule is stepped after every optimizer update,
with a 10,000-step horizon and minimum learning rate $10^{-5}$.
The prescribed schedule uses batches of 16, bfloat16 mixed precision, and gradient
checkpointing within encoder blocks. Its duration is 20 epochs, capped at
500 training batches and 100 validation batches per epoch.

The objective encourages agreement under the selected view transformations
while penalizing departures from the Gaussian projection target. Neither
term explicitly removes subject identity, acquisition-related information,
or artifacts shared across views. Whether the resulting representations
retain useful information under montage changes must therefore be
established by downstream evaluations and ablations.
